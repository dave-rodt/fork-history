History of Forks: Chapter 3
Bitcoin Gold (BTG), forked October 24, 2017
THE RENTABLE-HASHRATE PROBLEM

Bitcoin Gold vs. Bitcoin

Bitcoin Gold swapped SHA-256 for a GPU-friendly algorithm to fight ASIC-driven mining centralization. The same property that let ordinary GPUs mine it also let anyone rent enough hashpower to attack it, twice, for a fraction of what each attack took.

~? min read
01

Background

~1 min

By 2017, Bitcoin's original vision of "one CPU, one vote" was already a relic.

Mining had left the bedroom and moved into warehouses filled with ASICs, machines built for one job: mining Bitcoin. The economics favored whoever could buy the most hardware, secure the cheapest electricity, and operate at the largest scale.

At the center of that world was Bitmain, led by Jihan Wu. The company was both a dominant hardware manufacturer and a major mining pool operator. A miner could end up buying machines from the same company that was competing with them for blocks.

And the concentration was becoming hard to ignore. For long stretches, a handful of Chinese mining pools controlled a large share of Bitcoin's hash rate.

Then Bitcoin faced one of its fiercest political battles yet.

SegWit promised to change how Bitcoin handled transaction data, but some large miners resisted. Critics also pointed to AsicBoost, a technique that could provide compatible hardware with a significant efficiency advantage, one that SegWit would largely eliminate. Bitmain's chips supported the technique, although the company denied using it on Bitcoin.

The conflict exposed an uncomfortable possibility: the people with the greatest influence over Bitcoin's security could also have economic reasons to resist changes to the protocol.

SegWit activated in August 2017. But the battle had demonstrated something that was difficult to unsee:

Mining was not just about securing Bitcoin. It was also a source of power.

02

Why Change the Mining Algorithm?

~1 min

If the miners securing Bitcoin could influence which changes the network accepted, Bitcoin Gold asked a sharper question:

What if you made the existing miners obsolete?

The founders saw a structural problem. SHA-256 rewarded specialized hardware, large capital, and cheap electricity, conditions that favored industrial operators. Reforming that industry looked difficult. So they chose a different route: make the existing hardware useless.

By replacing SHA-256 with a different proof-of-work algorithm, Bitcoin Gold aimed to remove the advantage held by Bitcoin's specialized miners and give ordinary GPU miners a chance to compete.

The goal was not simply to create another version of Bitcoin. It was to change the economics of who could secure the network.

03

How Did It Happen?

~1 min

Led by Jack Liao of LightningASIC, Bitcoin Gold was announced and built in public during 2017.

The project chose Equihash, a memory-hard algorithm designed to favor GPUs over Bitcoin's specialized ASICs. The idea was straightforward: if Bitcoin's mining industry had become concentrated around specialized machines, changing the algorithm could open the door to a broader base of miners.

On October 24, 2017, after Bitcoin block 491,406, Bitcoin Gold took a snapshot of the Bitcoin blockchain. From that moment, the chains shared the same history but followed different mining rules. Every Bitcoin holder at the snapshot was entitled to BTG on a 1:1 basis.

But before public mining began, the project mined 8,000 blocks at minimum difficulty, producing roughly 100,000 BTG. The coins were placed in 4-of-6 multisig wallets as an "endowment." Critics simply called it a premine.

Public mining opened on November 13, 2017. Alongside Equihash, the chain launched with replay protection, new address prefixes (G and A), per-block difficulty adjustment, and the SegWit code already present in Bitcoin.

The fork was live. Bitcoin Gold had changed the rules of mining and was now putting its central idea to the test.

04

The Outcome

~2 min

The first challenge arrived almost immediately.

In May 2018, Bitmain announced an Equihash ASIC. The hardware Bitcoin Gold had tried to escape had followed it.

That same month, the network suffered 51% attacks. Attackers, likely using rented hash power, reorganized the chain and double-spent BTG. Bitcoin Gold responded on July 3, 2018 with a second hard fork, switching to Equihash-BTG to break the new ASICs and make hash-power rental harder to use.

The sequence revealed the first hard lesson of ASIC resistance: it is a moving target. Enough economic reward creates an incentive to build specialized hardware. Changing the algorithm can delay the process; it cannot erase the incentive.

The attacks revealed a second lesson. Making mining accessible does not automatically make a network secure. Bitcoin Gold had changed the hardware, but not the underlying economics. Cheap power still mattered. Scale still mattered. Hash power could still be concentrated or rented.

The same vulnerability returned in January 2020. Attackers carried out two deep reorganizations and double-spent roughly 7,000 BTG.

Then came another problem, this time closer to home.

The project had promised more decentralized mining, yet it had allocated 100,000 BTG to itself during the first 8,000 blocks. Whatever the intended use of the endowment, critics had an easy objection: decentralization is not only about who can mine. It is also about who receives the coins.

And eventually, even control of the project itself became contested.

Jack Liao stepped down from the Bitcoin Gold board in late 2018, leaving the remaining team to run the project. Years later, a dispute erupted over the project's website. In 2024, Liao took control of the bitcoingold.org domain, while the remaining team moved to a new domain. Each side offered a different account of what happened: the team said Liao had left and sold his holdings; Liao presented the takeover as a rebirth of the project.

There was something almost paradoxical about it.

A project created to reduce the concentration of power in Bitcoin's mining industry had ended up fighting over who controlled its own infrastructure.

And that brought the story back to Bitcoin.

The 2017 SegWit battle had shown that large miners could exert significant pressure on Bitcoin's development, but they did not have the final say. SegWit activated after months of conflict, with user-activated soft-fork pressure helping tip the balance.

Bitcoin Gold changed the miners, the hardware, and the algorithm. But Bitcoin's miners were never the only ones with power.

Bitcoin Gold changed who could mine, but it did not change how power works in a decentralized network.
