History of Forks: Chapter 4
Bitcoin BLAKE2b (XBT), forked August 8, 2026
THE NEWEST FORK IN THE SERIES

Bitcoin BLAKE2b: Three‑Month Retrospective

XBT is commonly used as a ticker for BTC by financial institutions such as Bloomberg, BitMEX, Kraken, and CoinShares. In this article, however, I use XBT to refer specifically to one of the two chains that emerged from the August 2026 Bitcoin fork, following disagreements over non-monetary data in blocks and later replacing SHA-256 with BLAKE2b as its proof-of-work (PoW) algorithm. BTC refers to the other chain, which did not adopt BIP-110 and continued using SHA-256 PoW.

Three months after the split, we examine what market and on-chain data reveal about the new chain’s first months.

01

The Background

~1.5 min

It started with a disagreement over what belongs in a Bitcoin block. It ended with a new PoW algorithm and a new chain.

To understand how that happened, we need to go back to the debate between Bitcoin Core and Bitcoin Knots.

Maintained by Luke Dashjr, Bitcoin Knots is an alternative Bitcoin node implementation. It follows the same consensus rules as Bitcoin Core but gives node operators more control over which transactions they relay and mine.

That distinction became increasingly important after the Ordinals wave in 2023.

Some Bitcoiners argued that inscriptions, large OP_RETURN outputs, and other non-monetary data were consuming block space, increasing node costs, and competing with financial transactions.

Knots became the main software associated with this position, while Bitcoin Core moved toward a more permissive relay policy.

The conflict escalated in 2025.

In May, Core announced that its next release would relay and mine OP_RETURN outputs larger than 80 bytes by default.S1 On June 6, 31 Core contributors published a joint statement defending a more permissive approach: if miners were allowed to include a transaction under consensus rules, nodes should generally relay it rather than impose additional restrictions.S2

Then came Core v30.0 in October.S30

It removed the traditional 83-byte OP_RETURN limit, raised the default -datacarriersize to 100,000 bytes, and allowed multiple data-carrier outputs per transaction.

For Luke Dashjr and other critics, this crossed an important line. Arbitrary data was no longer merely tolerated. Bitcoin’s transaction layer was becoming increasingly accommodating to it.

The reaction was significant. Knots’ share of reachable nodes grew from roughly 2% to more than 20% in late 2025.S3

What had started as a disagreement about transaction filtering had become a much bigger question:

What is Bitcoin block space for, and who gets to decide?

02

Why BIP-110?

~1 min

Its supporters wanted the data limits written into Bitcoin’s rules, not left to each node’s settings.

BIP-110, formally titled the Reduced Data Temporary Softfork, emerged from this conflict.S4

It first circulated as BIP-444 in late October 2025 and was later assigned BIP-110, with Dathon Ohm as its formal author. Luke Dashjr is widely credited with the original draft.S5

Its supporters had three main concerns.

  1. Spam and node costs

    Large amounts of arbitrary data could create artificial demand for block space, increase the cost of running a node, and make monetary transactions compete with non-financial data.

  2. Illegal content

    Bitcoin’s ledger is permanent. Supporters argued that allowing increasingly large data payloads could make it easier to embed illegal material, including child sexual abuse material (CSAM), creating potential legal and regulatory risks for node operators.

    Critics countered that such content could already be embedded through other mechanisms and that relay policy does not determine what miners ultimately include.

  3. Protocol governance

    Relay restrictions can be bypassed. A transaction rejected by ordinary nodes can still be submitted directly to miners.

    Supporters therefore argued that the restrictions needed to be enforced at the consensus level, including limits on new output scripts and OP_RETURN data.

The disagreement was no longer simply about spam.

It was about whether Bitcoin should primarily serve as a monetary network or a general-purpose data layer.

And, perhaps more importantly, whether changing node policy was enough, or whether changing the rules themselves was necessary.

03

How Did It Happen?

~3 min

Miners never came along. The chain split, then changed its PoW to survive on its own.

Key dates

  1. Mar 2026First block signals BIP-110
  2. Aug 8Chain splits at block 961,632
  3. Aug 28Last SHA-256 block on the BIP-110 chain
  4. Aug 30First BLAKE2b block, 961,640
  5. Sep 21Mining rewards locked for 45 days
  6. Oct 31Lock extended to 180 days

BIP-110 was designed as a temporary User-Activated Soft Fork (UASF). Users could voluntarily enforce its rules rather than wait for miners. It had a one-year duration and a 55% miner-signaling threshold for early activation.S4

The strategy was to make miners follow once enough users enforced the rules. But miner support never materialized. Although the first signaling block arrived in March, fewer than 1% of blocks signaled support through most of July.S6S7 The final period recorded just 51 blocks, or 2.53%, with no major mining pool backing the proposal.S8S9

User adoption was also uncertain. Estimates of BIP-110-capable nodes ranged from 2% to 8%, while broader estimates based on Knots adoption reached approximately 20%.S11S3 With limited evidence of economic support, miners had little incentive to signal.

The 2017 comparison

BIP-110 supporters sought to replicate BIP-148, the UASF that helped activate SegWit in 2017. But SegWit had broad support across the Bitcoin economy, unlike BIP-110, which lacked comparable backing from exchanges, businesses, and other economic actors. The proposals also had different technical objectives: SegWit addressed transaction malleability and scalability, while BIP-110 sought to restrict non-monetary data in blocks.

The comparison became controversial when Jonathan Bier discussed backchannel lobbying by BIP-148 supporters. Some BIP-110 proponents interpreted his comments1 as a confirmation that behind-the-scenes coordination helped explain BIP-148’s success, and why BIP-110 failed. Bier’s comments, however, do not establish that lobbying was decisive.

The split came on August 8. AntPool mined block 961,632 without signaling BIP-110.S10 Nodes that did not implement BIP-110 accepted it; enforcing nodes rejected it. When Ocean mined a compliant block 36 minutes later, two chains were operating under different rules.

More than 99% of the hashrate remained on the heaviest chain. The minority kept the mining difficulty but had almost no mining power. It produced just eight blocks in its first 20 days, with Ocean mining its last SHA-256 block on August 28.S20 It also initially lacked replay protection, meaning transactions could be valid on both chains. By mid-August, more than 400,000 transactions were waiting in its mempool.S12

The split raised a fundamental question:

Which chain is Bitcoin?

The two sides had very different answers.

XBT’s view Nodes decide what is valid. Nodes, not miners, decide which rules are valid. Hashpower selects among valid chains; it does not determine which rules are legitimate. With no effective opposition through a coordinated User-Resisted Soft Fork (URSF), BIP-110 supporters argued they were entitled to enforce the new rules. A chain that violates those rules is not Bitcoin to them, regardless of its hashrate.

BTC’s view The rules never changed. Bitcoin is the chain validated under the existing consensus rules, supported by the majority of users, exchanges, businesses, and miners. Those rules did not change on August 8, and over 99% of hashrate stayed. For BTC supporters, nodes continuing to enforce their existing rules was enough to establish which chain remained Bitcoin.

With BIP-110 unable to become a network-wide upgrade, its supporters needed another way to secure the chain with the least PoW. At a Knots meeting on August 10, Luke Dashjr, Kyle and Chris Guida divided development tasks covering the new block format, replay protection, and BIP-110 rules. A commit-reveal lottery on Testnet4 selected BLAKE2b on August 12.S13

The algorithm gave miners access to an existing secondary market of ASIC hardware, since it was already used by Siacoin, while rendering SHA-256 ASICs incompatible with XBT mining. The new format also introduced opt-in replay protection: specially signed transactions would be invalid on BTC, but normally signed transactions could still be replayed on XBT. Protection therefore worked in one direction and depended on users and wallets choosing to adopt it.S14S25

On August 30, SilentWave mined block 961,640, the first BLAKE2b block.S15 The final Knots release implementing the fork followed on September 2.S21

Mining centralization became the next concern. By September 8, approximately 17 PH/s of BLAKE2b hashrate was distributed across 27 pools, but Alphapool briefly controlled more than 50%.S16

On September 21, Knots PR #419 extended coinbase maturity from 100 to 6,480 blocks, approximately 45 days, for rewards generated between blocks 973,440 and 979,920.S17S26 The intention was to discourage short-term miners. On October 31, another rule extended maturity to 180 days, further increasing the cost of short-term mining.S27

The chain had changed its rules to escape the SHA-256 mining ecosystem. Now it was changing them again to address the risks of its own.

05

What Do Forks Tell Us?

~1.5 min

Every fork that fixes one problem risks creating another.

When a chain splits, two new networks are released into the same competitive environment. Both inherit history, code, and part of the original community, but neither automatically retains its security, liquidity, or economic weight. Each must prove itself under the pressures of miners, markets, developers, exchanges, and users.

The outcomes have varied. Ethereum’s response to The DAO showed that a community could coordinate a controversial change to its history and still become the dominant chain. Bitcoin Cash remains an active alternative more than nine years later, though far smaller than Bitcoin. Bitcoin Gold attempted to address mining centralization by changing its PoW algorithm; the experiment largely faded. XBT brought renewed attention to three longstanding questions: how Bitcoin should use its block space, how its mining network should be secured, and how much influence a single development team should have over the protocol. Whether changing transaction rules, the mining algorithm, and the development model can bring us closer to a truly global monetary network remains to be seen.

These trade-offs take different forms. Larger blocks can improve throughput while increasing the cost of running a node. Preserving immutability can protect principles while accepting short-term losses. Changing the mining algorithm can reset the hardware landscape while leaving the underlying economics of power and scale intact.

The debates behind the 2026 split are still live. Some argue that high fees and limited base-layer capacity remain unresolved. Others insist the base layer should stay as restrictive as possible. Mining-pool concentration remains a parallel concern on BTC, where three pools still control the majority of hashrate.S35

Having different rules is easy. Building a network that people actually use, secure, and value is much harder. Both chains will continue to face these questions. A chain that no longer has to answer them is a chain that no longer matters.
